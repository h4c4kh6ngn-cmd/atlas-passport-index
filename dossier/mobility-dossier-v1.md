# The Atlasify Mobility Dossier
### A practical guide to understanding — and improving — your passport power

*Draft v1. Written from Atlasify's own dataset (199 countries, verified and re-cited monthly). This version intentionally does not include country-by-country tax and residency chapters — see the note at the end on why, and what that means for scope.*

---

## 1. How to read your passport score

Your score on Atlasify is the number of destinations (out of 227 tracked) you can enter without arranging a visa in advance — this includes fully visa-free entry, visa-on-arrival, and electronic travel authorizations (eTA/ESTA-style). It does **not** mean "you can live and work there" — it means "you can show up."

Three numbers matter more than the raw score:

- **Your tier** (Elite 180+, Strong 140+, Moderate 100+, Limited 60+, Weak 40+, Critical <40) — this tells you how much *advance planning* international travel requires for you, which is the real practical cost of a weak passport.
- **EU/Schengen membership** — worth far more than its visa-free count suggests, because it includes the right to live and work in 27 countries, not just visit them.
- **US Visa Waiver Program (VWP) membership** — the single most commonly missing "connector" for otherwise-strong passports (see Section 3).

## 2. The legal routes to a second passport

There are two structurally different paths: **routes you qualify for by birthright or relationship**, and **routes you buy your way into**. Both are legal; they differ enormously in cost, timeline, and who's eligible.

### A. Routes based on who you are

| Route | How it works | Typical timeline | Who it's for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Citizenship by descent** | A parent or grandparent was a citizen of another country | Months, mostly paperwork | People with recent foreign ancestry — Italy, Ireland, and Poland have among the most generous descent rules in the world |
| **Citizenship by marriage** | Marrying a citizen of another country | 1–5 years depending on country | Applies case-by-case; most countries require a genuine marriage and a residency period, not just the certificate |
| **Naturalization** | Living in a country long enough, then applying | 3–10 years depending on country | The slowest but cheapest route in money terms — Portugal (5 years) and Argentina (2 years, one of the fastest in the world) are notably quick by naturalization standards |
| **Residency-by-investment → citizenship** | A residence permit (often via property purchase or a fund investment) that *can* lead to citizenship after a waiting period | 5–10 years to citizenship | Golden Visa-style programs (Greece, Portugal historically) — slower than CBI but usually far cheaper |

None of these require six figures. All of them require time, which is the actual price.

### B. Citizenship-by-investment (CBI) — buying a passport outright

These programs grant citizenship directly, usually within a few months, in exchange for a donation or investment. This is the fast, expensive route — and the one most "second passport" marketing is actually selling. Here's what's real and currently verified in Atlasify's own dataset:

| Country | Score | From | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Saint Kitts and Nevis** | 157 | ~$250,000 (donation) | The original CBI program, running since 1984 — the longest track record of any program on this list |
| **Antigua and Barbuda** | 155 | ~$230,000 | Includes visa-free Schengen and UK access |
| **Saint Lucia** | 146 | ~$240,000 | Also available via government bonds, not just donation |
| **Grenada** | 148 | ~$230,000 | Unique: visa-free China access, plus an E-2 investor treaty with the US — relevant if the goal is eventually doing business in the US |
| **Dominica** | 145 | ~$200,000 | The best-known budget option |
| **Turkey** | 113 | ~$400,000 (real estate) | The world's best-selling investment passport by volume — but Schengen still requires a visa, which undercuts the main reason most people want a second passport |
| **Vanuatu** | 89 | — | The fastest program in the world (~2 months) — but it lost visa-free Schengen access after launch. Included here as a cautionary example: CBI passports can and do lose value after you buy them, because visa-free access is a bilateral political relationship, not a fixed product feature. |

**The one-sentence takeaway:** a CBI passport is worth buying when it fills a specific, identifiable gap in your existing mobility (see Section 3) — not as a status purchase. Turkey and Vanuatu above are the clearest illustrations of why: both are popular, both are legitimate, and both deliver meaningfully less than buyers often assume going in.

## 3. The one gap most people should actually check first

Before spending anything, check one specific thing: **does your current passport have US Visa Waiver Program (VWP) access?**

It's the most common "missing piece" in an otherwise strong passport, because VWP membership doesn't track cleanly with overall score or EU membership — the UAE only gained it in 2024 after over a decade of diplomacy, and Qatar is (as of this dataset) the only Gulf country to have it at all. If your passport is otherwise Strong or Elite tier but lacks VWP, that single gap is very often cheaper and faster to solve directly (longer-validity visas, Global Entry-adjacent programs, or a strategically chosen second residency) than via a full CBI purchase.

## 4. What this dossier doesn't cover yet, and why

The original pitch for this dossier promised country-by-country tax and residency analysis, and a "matrix" showing which two-passport combination covers a given percentage of the world. Being direct about the current state:

- **Country-by-country tax and residency chapters aren't included in this v1.** Tax and immigration law is jurisdiction-specific, changes often, and getting it wrong isn't a content-quality problem — it's the kind of mistake that costs a reader real money or a rejected application. That needs either licensed local expertise per country or a much more rigorous sourcing process than a single-pass write-up can honestly claim. It's a real "v2" project, not a shortcut.
- **A precise "two-passport coverage matrix" isn't buildable from Atlasify's current dataset.** Atlasify tracks each passport's *total* visa-free destination count, not the full bilateral matrix (which of the 227 destinations, specifically, each of the 199 passports can access). Computing an accurate combined-coverage percentage for a given pair of passports needs that full matrix, which Atlasify doesn't hold licensing for in that granular form. Advertising a specific coverage percentage without that data would mean making the number up — so Section 3 above gives the same underlying advice (identify your specific access gap) honestly, without a fabricated statistic attached to it.
